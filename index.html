The Environmental Cost of Convenience

When a customer clicks "buy" and picks same-day delivery, the choice feels costless. The delivery fee, if there is one, is small, the parcel turns up at the door, and the whole transaction feels clean and efficient.

Behind it, though, is a physical journey: a vehicle, a driver, fuel burned and emissions produced. The last mile, the final leg from a depot to the customer, is the most carbon-intensive part of that journey. Vans are often only partly full, they stop frequently, they get stuck in urban traffic, and parcels that cannot be handed over have to be delivered again. Together these make last-mile delivery a significant and growing source of greenhouse gas emissions in cities.

In Singapore, transport accounts for about 15 percent of national greenhouse gas emissions, and last-mile delivery vehicles make up a meaningful share of that. As e-commerce keeps growing, the emissions from deliveries will grow with it, unless the sector changes how it operates.

Diesel vs Electric: The Vehicle Comparison

The most direct way to cut the carbon footprint of last-mile delivery is to replace diesel vehicles with electric ones.

A diesel delivery van produces roughly 180 to 220 grams of CO2 for every kilometre it drives. An equivalent electric van produces no tailpipe emissions at all. Electricity still has to be generated to charge it, and in Singapore that comes mainly from natural gas, but even counting that, an electric van's emissions over its whole life are significantly lower than a diesel van's.

Electric vans also cost less to run. Electricity is cheaper than diesel per kilometre, and electric motors have far fewer moving parts than combustion engines, so maintenance costs less. The main financial barriers are the purchase price, which is typically 30 to 50 percent higher than a diesel equivalent at current prices, and the cost of installing charging equipment at depots.

As battery technology improves and more vehicles are produced, that price gap is expected to narrow significantly over the next five to ten years, which will make the switch easier to justify financially.

Beyond the Vehicle: Operational Sustainability

Switching to electric vehicles matters, but it only deals with one side of the problem. The other major lever is operational: reducing the total number of kilometres driven, whatever powers the vehicle. Every kilometre not driven is a kilometre of emissions avoided.

Several strategies do this directly:

  • Route optimisation plans more efficient routes, so the total distance driven falls.
  • Better vehicle utilisation means vans leave the depot as full as possible, so fewer vehicles make the same number of deliveries.
  • Fewer failed deliveries come from collection and delivery points (CDPs), delivery time-window notifications and good communication with customers. Each avoided redelivery avoids its emissions too.
  • Consolidating deliveries means several logistics companies coordinate and share vehicles on the same routes, which can sharply cut the number of vans on the road in an area.

All of these can be done now, with existing technology, without waiting for the vehicle fleet to be replaced.

Delivery Consolidation and Shared Infrastructure

Delivery consolidation is one of the biggest opportunities for cutting last-mile emissions, and one of the least used. On a typical day in a Singapore public housing (HDB) estate, vans from SingPost, Ninja Van, J&T Express, DHL and Lalamove may all be delivering to the same blocks, each only partly loaded and each making its own stops.

If those parcels were loaded onto one shared vehicle and delivered together, the number of vehicle trips to the estate could fall by 60 to 80 percent. Emissions, congestion and parking pressure would fall with it.

The barriers are real, though. Competing logistics companies are reluctant to share vehicles and infrastructure. Tracking parcels and assigning liability get more complicated when several carriers' goods are on one vehicle. And the technology to coordinate carriers needs investment and common standards.

Singapore's government has recognised the opportunity. The Locker Alliance programme aims to create a shared CDP locker network that any carrier can use, which is one step towards consolidation. The Singapore Logistics Association has also been exploring frameworks for urban consolidation centres, where parcels from many carriers are sorted and loaded onto shared last-mile vehicles before they enter residential areas.

The Role of Policy

Sustainable last-mile delivery at scale needs more than individual company decisions. It needs policies that create the right incentives and remove barriers to change.

Singapore's Green Plan 2030 sets national targets for reducing emissions across all sectors, including transport. For commercial vehicles, the Land Transport Authority's Commercial Vehicle Emissions Scheme gives companies financial incentives to retire older, more polluting vehicles and replace them with cleaner ones, including electric vans.

The government has also invested in charging infrastructure for commercial vehicles, because a lack of convenient charging points is one of the main barriers to logistics companies adopting electric vehicles. On the operational side, the Logistics Industry Transformation Map provides funding and support for companies to adopt technology such as route optimisation software, CDP infrastructure and fleet management systems, which improves both efficiency and sustainability.

These levers work together. Vehicle standards push companies towards cleaner fleets, infrastructure investment makes the change practical, and support for technology helps companies run those fleets more efficiently.

Consumer Behaviour and Sustainability

Technology and policy are important, but customer behaviour is also a significant lever.

The choices customers make about delivery speed, time windows and returns directly affect the carbon footprint of their purchases:

  • Same-day delivery needs dedicated vehicles that are often not full.
  • Narrow time windows make routing less efficient.
  • Impulse purchases with high return rates create extra trips to collect returns.
  • Expecting free delivery removes the price signal that might otherwise encourage customers to choose more sustainable options.

Some e-commerce platforms are starting to experiment with "eco delivery" options, where customers choose slower, consolidated delivery in exchange for a discount or reward. Research suggests that a meaningful share of customers will pick the more sustainable option if it is clearly presented and the trade-off is reasonable.

In Singapore, platforms including Shopee and Lazada have introduced carbon offset programmes and eco-packaging options, although these are so far more symbolic than transformative. A more impactful change would be to show customers the true environmental cost of each delivery option at the point of purchase. That is starting to appear in Europe but has not yet become standard in Southeast Asia.

Putting It Together: A Sustainable Last-Mile System

A truly sustainable last-mile delivery system needs change at several levels at the same time. No single technology or policy fix is enough; it takes a transformation of the whole system.

  • Vehicle level: moving from diesel to electric vans and, for short urban routes, to cargo e-bikes and eventually autonomous robots, lowers the emissions of each delivery.
  • Operational level: route optimisation, higher vehicle utilisation, fewer failed deliveries and consolidation reduce the total kilometres driven.
  • Infrastructure level: shared CDP networks and urban consolidation centres let the whole logistics ecosystem work more efficiently.
  • Policy level: emissions standards, infrastructure investment and support for technology create the conditions for change.
  • Consumer level: clearer information about the environmental cost of delivery choices can shift demand towards more sustainable options.

No one of these is enough on its own. Together, they point to a last-mile system that can keep serving Singapore's growing e-commerce demand without a matching rise in emissions and congestion.

Summary

Last-mile delivery is a significant and growing source of urban emissions, driven by low vehicle utilisation, frequent stops, congestion and failed deliveries.

Replacing diesel vans with electric vehicles lowers the emissions of each delivery, and there is a clear financial case over the vehicle's lifetime despite the higher upfront cost. Operational strategies (route optimisation, higher utilisation, fewer failed deliveries and consolidation) reduce the total kilometres driven, whatever the vehicle type.

Singapore's policy framework, including the Green Plan 2030, the Commercial Vehicle Emissions Scheme and the Logistics Industry Transformation Map, provides incentives and support for the change. Consumer behaviour, particularly choices about delivery speed and returns, is an underused lever that platforms are beginning to use.

Sustainable last-mile delivery needs change at all of these levels at once.
